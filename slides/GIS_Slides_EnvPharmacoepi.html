Mapping Antimicrobial Resistance Across U.S. States

Welcome

We are going to build a small but complete GIS workflow in R, using real CDC surveillance data:

  1. ARPSP_MDR-Ecoli---Percent-Resistant-in-2024.csv — the percent of multidrug-resistant (MDR) E. coli isolates among healthcare-associated infections, by state, from CDC’s Antibiotic Resistance & Patient Safety Portal (ARPSP), 2024.
  2. BEAM_Dashboard_-_Report_Data_20260804.csv — CDC enteric bacteria surveillance isolates (Salmonella, E. coli/STEC, Campylobacter, Shigella, Vibrio) drawn from animal, food, human, and environmental sources, reported monthly since 2018. We will use the environmental-source Salmonella records as a proxy exposure / surveillance-effort.
  3. 2024-SIR-ACH.xlsx — CDC NHSN Standardized Infection Ratio (SIR) report for acute care hospitals, 2024. You will use this yourself in Part 8.

None of these files contain a shapefile, a latitude, or a longitude. That is deliberate. Most pharmacoepidemiology environmental exposure data you will encounter in practice — surveillance reports, claims data aggregated for privacy, vital statistics — arrives as tables tied to an administrative area (state, county, ZIP) and to a time period (month, year).

Learning objectives

By the end, you will be able to:

  • Import and clean real, messy public-health surveillance data in R
  • Obtain U.S. state boundary polygons and understand what a coordinate reference system (CRS) is doing for you
  • Perform an attribute join — attaching non-spatial data to spatial geometry by a shared key — the single most common GIS operation you will do in this field
  • Build choropleth (thematic) maps with ggplot2 + sf
  • Build small-multiples map panels to show how a distribution changes across years and across seasons, and understand why the color scale has to be shared across panels for the comparison to be fair
  • Recognize seasonality in area-level surveillance data using both a simple distribution plot and a mapped version of the same pattern
  • Name the specific ways area-level GIS analysis can mislead you in environmental pharmacoepidemiology (MAUP, ecological fallacy, surveillance effort bias) — and recognize them in your own results

states_sf is an sf object: an ordinary data frame with one extra column, geometry, holding the polygon boundary for each state. Everything you already know about dplyr::filter(), mutate(), and left_join() still works — that is the whole appeal of sf.

ggplot(states_sf) +
  geom_sf() +
  theme_minimal() +
  labs(title = "U.S. state boundaries (no data joined yet)")

For the rest of this exercise we’ll restrict to the contiguous 48 states + DC. Alaska, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, and the outlying territories are real and important, but they sit far outside the CONUS bounding box and would either get cut off or force us to shrink every other state to fit them on the same simple map. (tigris::shift_geometry() can reposition them into inset boxes if you need a true all-50-states map later — worth knowing exists, out of scope for today.) For a first pass at small-multiples mapping, keeping the geography simple lets us focus on the actual skill: comparing panels to each other.

conus_codes <- setdiff(c(state.abb, "DC"), c("AK", "HI"))

states_conus <- states_sf %>%
  filter(stusps %in% conus_codes) %>%
  st_transform(5070)   # Albers Equal Area Conic — standard projected CRS for CONUS

CRS check-in. tigris gives you data in geographic coordinates (NAD83, unprojected — degrees of latitude/longitude). We just projected to EPSG:5070, an equal-area projection built for the contiguous U.S. Projection matters here for two concrete reasons: (1) ggplot2 will draw a nicer, less distorted map, and (2) any analysis that depends on area or distance (which ours doesn’t today, but often will) requires a projected CRS to be correct. Always ask “what CRS is this in, and does that CRS make sense for what I’m about to compute?”

Part 3 — Attribute join

This is the step that turns a spreadsheet into a map. We attach our two non-spatial tables to states_conus by matching a shared key: full state name for the ARPSP data, two-letter abbreviation for the BEAM data.

map_data <- states_conus %>%
  left_join(ecoli, by = c("name" = "state")) %>%
  left_join(beam_env_clean, by = c("stusps" = "state")) %>%
  mutate(env_isolates = coalesce(env_isolates, 0))  # no report = 0 isolates, not missing

Stop and check your join. A silent join failure — rows that should have matched but didn’t, because of a trailing space, a “St.” vs “Saint”, a different case — is the single most common source of a blank or wrong-looking choropleth map. Before you ever trust a joined map, run:

map_data %>%
  st_drop_geometry() %>%
  filter(is.na(percent_resistant)) %>%
  select(name, stusps)
     name stusps

If that returns states you expected to have data, go back and find the mismatch. If it’s empty, your join key worked.

A note on missing data before we go further

Run summary(map_data$percent_resistant) again and look for NA's. One state — Wyoming — has a missing value in the ARPSP source data (not a join error). Small states often have their rates suppressed in public surveillance data when the underlying case count is too small to report reliably without a risk of re-identifying a patient. This is a real and recurring feature of small-area public health data, not a data-cleaning mistake to hide.

Part 5 — Expanding across years: small multiples of environmental surveillance

A single choropleth is a snapshot. Most of the interesting questions in environmental pharmacoepidemiology are about change — did something increase, decrease, or stay flat? The standard way to show that spatially is a small-multiples panel: the same map, repeated once per time period, laid out in a grid so the eye can compare them directly.

Let’s rebuild the BEAM environmental Salmonella extract, this time keeping every full year from 2018 through 2025 (we’ll drop 2026 — it’s only partially reported as of today, and a partial year next to eight full years would make the most recent panel look like a decline that isn’t real).

beam_env_years <- beam %>%
  filter(source_type == "Environment", pathogen == "Salmonella",
         year >= 2018, year <= 2025,
         state %in% valid_state_codes) %>%
  group_by(state, year) %>%
  summarize(isolates = sum(number_of_isolates, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop")

Every CONUS state should get one row for every year, even the years it reported nothing — an absent row and a reported zero mean different things once you’re building a panel, and only one of them is correct here.

beam_panel <- expand_grid(
  stusps = states_conus$stusps,
  year   = 2018:2025
) %>%
  left_join(beam_env_years, by = c("stusps" = "state", "year")) %>%
  mutate(isolates = coalesce(isolates, 0))

nrow(beam_panel)   # 49 states x 8 years
[1] 392
panel_sf <- states_conus %>%
  select(stusps, name, geometry) %>%
  left_join(beam_panel, by = "stusps")
ggplot(panel_sf) +
  geom_sf(aes(fill = isolates), color = "white", linewidth = 0.05) +
  facet_wrap(~ year, ncol = 4) +
  scale_fill_viridis_c(name = "Environmental\nSalmonella\nisolates",
                        limits = c(0, max(panel_sf$isolates))) +
  theme_void(base_size = 9) +
  theme(strip.text = element_text(face = "bold")) +
  labs(title = "Environmentally-sourced Salmonella isolates by state, 2018-2025",
       subtitle = "CDC BEAM enteric bacteria surveillance",
       caption = "Source: BEAM Dashboard")

Why the limits = c(0, max(panel_sf$isolates)) matters. Every panel here shares one color scale, built from the range of all eight years combined. If you instead mapped each year separately with its own default scale, a state with 5 isolates in a low year could look just as “dark” as a state with 50 isolates in a high year — same color, wildly different magnitude. A shared scale is what makes a small-multiples panel an honest comparison instead of eight unrelated pictures that happen to share an axis. This is arguably the single most common way choropleth series mislead a reader, and it’s a one-line fix.

Discussion prompts:

  2. Do any years look like a sharp jump or drop for many states at once (rather than one state)? A change that shows up broadly, in the same year, across unrelated states, is more likely a reporting or methods change than eight independent outbreaks.

Part 6 — Is there a seasonal pattern?

Small multiples don’t have to be organized by year — the same technique works for any repeating unit of time. Here we’ll look at season, using all eight years pooled together so each panel has enough data behind it to be worth looking at.

6a. Start non-spatial: what does the distribution look like?

Before mapping anything, look at the plain distribution. Pooling across 2018-2025 gives us enough isolates per month to see a shape, where any single year would mostly be noise.

monthly_totals <- beam %>%
  filter(source_type == "Environment", pathogen == "Salmonella",
         year >= 2018, year <= 2025,
         state %in% valid_state_codes) %>%
  group_by(month) %>%

ggplot(monthly_totals, aes(x = factor(month), y = isolates)) +

6b. Now map the same idea

Group the same eight years of data into four seasons per state, so we can see whether the pattern in 6a is broadly shared across the country or driven by a handful of states.

season_levels <- c("Winter (Dec-Feb)", "Spring (Mar-May)",
                    "Summer (Jun-Aug)", "Fall (Sep-Nov)")

beam_season <- beam %>%
  filter(source_type == "Environment", pathogen == "Salmonella",
         year >= 2018, year <= 2025,
         state %in% valid_state_codes) %>%
  mutate(season = case_when(
    month %in% c(12, 1, 2)  ~ "Winter (Dec-Feb)",
    month %in% c(3, 4, 5)   ~ "Spring (Mar-May)",
    month %in% c(6, 7, 8)   ~ "Summer (Jun-Aug)",
    month %in% c(9, 10, 11) ~ "Fall (Sep-Nov)"
  )) %>%
  group_by(state, season) %>%
  summarize(isolates = sum(number_of_isolates, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop")

season_panel <- expand_grid(stusps = states_conus$stusps, season = season_levels) %>%
  left_join(beam_season, by = c("stusps" = "state", "season")) %>%
  mutate(isolates = coalesce(isolates, 0),
         season = factor(season, levels = season_levels))

season_sf <- states_conus %>%
  select(stusps, geometry) %>%
  left_join(season_panel, by = "stusps")
ggplot(season_sf) +
  geom_sf(aes(fill = isolates), color = "white", linewidth = 0.05) +

Part 7 — Linking the environmental signal to the clinical outcome

Now the environmental pharmacoepi question: is a state’s environmental surveillance signal for Salmonella associated with its clinical MDR E. coli burden? We’ll use the full 2018-2025 total this time as we just saw in parts 5-6 just a single year can be noisy.

env_total <- beam_panel %>%
  group_by(stusps) %>%
  summarize(env_isolates_total = sum(isolates), .groups = "drop")

sum(env_total$env_isolates_total == 0)   # states with zero isolates, 2018-2025 combined

Discussion of the exercise. Work through it as a group.

  1. Is there a correlation? Is it what you expected? Compare the number of zero-isolate states here (pooling eight years) to the single-year 2024 map from Part 4 — pooling helped, but did it fully solve the surveillance-effort problem, or just shrink it?
  2. This is still surveillance/detection bias: an “exposure” built from surveillance counts is measuring exposure and testing effort at the same time, and you generally can’t fully separate the two from counts alone.
  3. Both variables here are aggregated to the state level, an administrative boundary chosen for governance, not for epidemiology. Two pieces of jargon worth knowing cold for this field:
    • Ecological fallacy: even if the correlation above is real, it tells you nothing about individual patients — you cannot conclude that a specific person’s resistant infection was caused by environmental exposure just because their state shows an association.
    • Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP): had we aggregated to counties, HHS regions, or watersheds instead of states, both the magnitude and even the direction of this relationship could change, simply from redrawing the boundaries — with no change to the underlying reality.
  4. If you were designing a real study on this question, what exposure variable would you actually want, and at what spatial and temporal resolution?

Wrap-up: taking this back to your own research

A few closing questions to carry out of the room:

  • Everything today used pre-aggregated, area-level administrative data. What would change in this workflow if you instead had point-level data — patient addresses, well locations, facility coordinates? (Think about buffers, distance-to-nearest-source, and kernel density instead of choropleths.)
  • Where in your own work do you currently use a “zone” (ZIP code, county, census tract, state) mostly because it’s the unit your data happens to come in, rather than because it’s the right scale for the exposure mechanism you care about?
  • Surveillance effort bias showed up clearly in Parts 5-7. Where might it be hiding, less visibly, in a dataset you already use?
